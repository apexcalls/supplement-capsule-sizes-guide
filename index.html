Why Supplement Capsules Come in Sizes That Make No Sense

Anyone who has taken supplements has wondered about it. One capsule is the size of a grain of rice; the next is the size of a small torpedo. Two products with the same dose can come in wildly different capsule sizes, and a capsule that looks half empty can contain exactly what the label promises. Capsule size is one of the least understood parts of a supplement label, and it is worth understanding, because size tells you something about what is inside — just not what most people assume.

The first thing to know is that capsule size is set by volume, not by dose. A capsule holds a fixed amount of powder or liquid, and the manufacturer fills it with whatever the formula requires: the active ingredient plus fillers, binders, flow agents and sometimes flavourings. A 500 mg dose of a dense mineral may fit into a small capsule, while a 500 mg dose of a fluffy botanical extract needs a much larger one. Two capsules with the same headline dose can therefore be very different sizes, and neither is more potent than the other. Judging a product by the size of its capsule is like judging a book by the size of its cover.

The second thing to know is that a capsule that looks half empty is usually not underfilled. Capsules are filled by volume, and powders settle. A capsule filled to the brim at the factory can look partly empty after weeks of vibration in shipping, as the powder compacts at one end. The weight is what matters, and reputable manufacturers check fill weight, not appearance. If a capsule rattles, that is normal. If a capsule is visibly cracked, leaking, or stuck to its neighbours, that is a storage problem, and the bottle should go back.

Size also reflects the form of the ingredient. Oils need soft gel capsules, which are larger and smoother than the hard capsules used for powders. Some ingredients are hygroscopic — they absorb moisture from the air — and need a larger capsule with room for a desiccant or a protective coating. Others are simply bulky: a gram of dried herb takes up far more space than a gram of a concentrated extract. A label-checking guide explains how to read the form of an ingredient from the panel, and that form often explains the capsule size better than the dose does.

There is one size signal that does matter. If the same brand's capsules suddenly change size without any change to the label, something in the formula has changed: a different filler, a different supplier, a different manufacturing process. The dose on the label may be identical, but the product is not. This is one of the quiet ways formulas change without announcement, and it is worth noticing. Capsule size is packaging, not potency. A small capsule can hold a strong dose, a large capsule can hold mostly filler, and a half-empty-looking capsule can be exactly right. What matters is the dose per serving, the form of the ingredient, and whether the label lets you check both. Learn to read those, and the size of the capsule stops being a mystery — it becomes one more small piece of evidence about the company that made it.
